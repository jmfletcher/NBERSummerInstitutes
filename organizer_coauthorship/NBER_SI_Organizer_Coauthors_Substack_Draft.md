# Do NBER Summer Institute organizers put their own papers on the program?

### Usually no. Not never. And a few programs look different.

Every July, NBER Summer Institute agendas fill up with working papers. The organizers are named on the program. The authors are named on the papers.

So you can ask a narrow question without turning it into a morality play:

**How often does a paper on a Summer Institute program list one of that program’s organizers as an author?**

---

## What I counted

For each research program-year, take the organizers listed for that meeting. Count a paper if at least one of those people is also an author. Divide by the number of research papers on the agenda.

Discussants don’t count. Panels and lectures don’t count. Each paper counts once.

That is a blunt measure on purpose. It does not tell you *how* the paper got on the program. It does not capture soft influence—students, frequent coauthors, friends of friends. It only answers: **same organizer, same program, same year, on the author line.**

---

## The headline

**2000–2019:** about **3.8%** of papers (364 of 9,601), using the public Rose–Opolot–Georg Summer Institute archive.

**2020–2026:** about **2.4%** of papers (105 of 4,322), from NBER’s printable agendas for 339 of 342 in-scope program-years.

That second number is a **paper-weighted average**: organizer-authored papers divided by all papers across programs that year—not an unweighted average of program shares. Large meetings count for more.

Year by year, the weighted share mostly sits around **1.9%–2.6%** from 2020 through 2025, then jumps to about **4.2%** in the 2026 agendas (as posted).

Two facts matter more than the average.

**Most meetings have none.** In the recent window, only about one in five program-years has even a single organizer-authored paper. The median is zero.

**Programs differ.** Public Economics is the clear high end in the recent data—on the order of **13%** of PE papers. Labor, health, development, and real estate run higher than average. Large meetings such as Monetary Economics, Urban, Impulse and Propagation, and several finance/macro programs sit at or near zero on this measure.

The typical picture is not organizers loading the agenda with their own work. It is usually none, occasionally one, rarely a cluster—and a few programs that look different.

---

## What that does and does not mean

A low rate can mean several things at once. Organizers may have already presented the paper elsewhere. They may prefer to give scarce slots to juniors. Or the call for papers may simply filter most organizer submissions out.

A non-zero rate is not, by itself, a scandal. Organizers are active researchers in the field. Sometimes the right paper for the room is their paper.

If you imagined Summer Institute as a venue where organizers routinely platform their own work, the 2000–2025 record does not support that. If you imagined a hard norm of zero, it does not support that either.

**Roughly one paper in forty** over 2020–2026 has an organizer on the author list—closer to one in fifty before 2026, and closer to one in twenty-five in the 2000–2019 archive. Most meetings have none.

That is the descriptive fact. What the rate *should* be is a separate argument.

---

*Data: 2000–2019 from the [NBER Summer Institutes archive](https://github.com/Michael-E-Rose/NBERSummerInstitutes); 2020–2026 from NBER printable agendas (2026 agendas as posted), with organizer rosters from published SI program lists. Three CRIW pre-conferences in 2023–2025 remain uncaptured. Figures: program series sized by papers, plus a paper-weighted average. Files in `NBER_SI_Organizer_Coauthors/`.*
